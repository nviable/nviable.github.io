## Case narrative

### Problem

Off-the-shelf deepfake detectors are black boxes. Journalists, intelligence analysts, and investigators need explainable cues, provenance context, and interfaces that match how they already work under time pressure — not a single probability bar they are asked to trust or distrust on faith.

### Constraints

- Stakeholders span newsrooms, government, and academia, each with different threat models
- Models and attack surfaces evolve faster than traditional release cycles
- Human-in-the-loop judgment must stay central to avoid automation bias
- Funding and compliance requirements span multiple sponsors

### Approach

Mixed-methods research — interviews, diary studies, workshops, and usability tests — drives requirements and iteration. High-fidelity Figma prototypes are tested with practitioners before engineering commits. The stack combines Flask/React services with PyTorch/Keras models and Dockerized components, and the design surfaces evidence across signals (intra-frame, inter-frame, audio, speaker identity) in a forensic-style interface rather than a single verdict.

### Key decisions

**Prioritize an explainable, multi-signal UI over a single authenticity score** — Expert users need to justify conclusions to editors and, sometimes, to courts; layered evidence reduces harmful over-trust in one number. Alternatives considered: a minimal UI wrapping a single classifier output; a fully automated moderation pipeline.

**Co-design with journalists and analysts from the first prototype** — Workflow fit and trust matter as much as raw model accuracy; field studies surfaced needs no lab study would catch. Alternatives considered: ship a demo first and collect feedback post-launch; rely only on academic peer review for validation.

### Tech stack

Python, Flask, React, PyTorch, Keras, Figma, Docker.

### Impact

The DeFake Project has grown from an idea into a sustained, interdisciplinary collaboration of roughly 18 researchers across four universities, supported by Knight, NSF, DoD, and Omidyar. It anchors a family of related efforts: the analytic interface, [Varuna](/projects/varuna) (an ontology-driven forensics platform), [Dungeons & Deepfakes](/projects/dungeons-and-deepfakes) (scenario-based verification training), and [We.DeFake](/projects/we-defake) (a federated collaboration community).

The research program has produced peer-reviewed work at CHI, SOUPS, CCS, and IEEE venues (see the related publications below), and the tool and its findings have reached both practitioner and public audiences — through coverage by CNET, Spectrum News, and the Columbia Journalism Review, and through direct engagement with government and standards communities, including a DARPA briefing on the project's media-forensics ontology.

### Learnings

- Usable security for media integrity means designing for doubt, calibration, and audit trails — not just accuracy
- Trust is earned at the interface: practitioners adopt detection tools when the evidence and its limits are legible, not when a score is simply high
- Cross-institution roadmaps need explicit stakeholder alignment and shared definitions of done
