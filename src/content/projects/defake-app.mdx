## Overview

The DeFake Project builds deepfake detection tools that people who verify media for a living can actually use. The project is based at the ESL Global Cybersecurity Institute at Rochester Institute of Technology. It works with partners at the University of Mississippi School of Journalism and New Media and at Michigan State University. Matthew Wright is the principal investigator. I founded the project and lead it.

According to RIT, the team formed in 2019, when Wright and the newly formed DeFake team answered a call from the Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence Initiative to build a deepfake detector. RIT reports more than $2 million in funding from the National Science Foundation and the Knight Foundation, along with a 2023 grant from the U.S. Department of Defense. The project was one of the winners of the AI and the News Open Challenge, which the Berkman Klein Center announced in March 2019 as having seven winners (defake.app says eight).

The project has two threads that inform each other. Detection research asks which signals reveal manipulation and how well they survive real-world conditions. User research asks how journalists, intelligence analysts, and investigators decide whether media is authentic, and what a tool must show them to support that decision. DeFake.app is where the two threads meet.

The research has appeared at ACM CHI, ACM CCS, USENIX SOUPS, IEEE JSTSP, and IEEE IJCB. Team members also co-authored *Fake-Checking: A Journalist's Guide to Deepfakes* (Routledge, 2025).

## Research

### Detection research

The DeFake Project started with one question. Can deepfake detection be useful to the people who verify media for a living? Our early work focused on detectors that hold up outside the lab, where they face unseen generation methods, social-media compression, and adversarial edits.

Our first prototype paired a detector that worked within and across datasets with an interface designed for journalists (ACM CCS 2019 poster). We then built models that combine spatial and temporal cues:

- Recurrent convolutional structures with entropy-based loss functions, which detect both video deepfakes and audio spoofing (IEEE JSTSP 2020).
- Facial edge maps and dense optical flow, which expose inconsistencies across space and time (IEEE IJCB 2020).

We also attacked sequence-based detectors with adversarial perturbations to measure how easily they can be fooled (ACM Workshop on Security Implications of Deepfakes and Cheapfakes, 2022). With collaborators at Michigan State University, we studied visual explanations for video models (BMVC 2021) and open-set face forgery detection (IEEE FG 2026).

Better models were only part of the problem. A detector that outputs one probability gives an expert nothing to defend in front of an editor or a court. Since then the research has moved toward explainable, multi-signal evidence. That means separate signals for:

- frame-level artifacts
- temporal consistency
- audio
- speaker identity

These signals are organized under a digital media forensics ontology, so each result maps to a specific kind of manipulation.

### User research

We design with practitioners, not for them. RIT's Cybersecurity department describes the tool as built on interviews with 46 journalists and 30 forensic analysts. Our studies include the following.

- **Journalists.** Formative interviews with journalists covered how they see deepfakes, how they verify media now, and what they expect from a detection tool (USENIX SOUPS 2020 poster, also presented at Computation + Journalism 2020).
- **Journalists under pressure.** Dungeons & Deepfakes put 24 U.S. journalists, from national broadcast networks to local print media, into scenario-based role-play in a high-stakes newsroom. They had access to detection tools and were free to choose their own verification steps. They verified carefully but sometimes leaned too heavily on the detector's output (ACM CHI 2024).
- **Intelligence analysts.** Interviews with 30 practitioners from the U.S. Intelligence Community showed that analysts need a comprehensive, explainable tool that supports report writing. That finding led to an analyst-centered design and a digital media forensics ontology (ACM CHI 2025).
- **Cross-cultural newsrooms.** We compared how journalists in the United States and Bangladesh adopt detection tools, where the tools fit in their verification workflow, and where automation bias appears (chapter in *PROMISE: PROMoting AI's Safe usage for Elections*, Springer, 2025).
- **Usability.** We run diary studies, workshops, and usability tests on high-fidelity prototypes before engineering starts. The Dungeons & Deepfakes method also serves as a basis for scenario-based verification training.

Across these studies, one finding kept coming back. Practitioners trust a tool when they can see its evidence and its limits, not when its score is high.

DeFake.app turns this research into a working tool. It is available to collaborating journalists, forensic analysts, and law enforcement personnel on request.

**Submitting media.** A user uploads a video (MP4, MOV, or WebM) or pastes a YouTube or X post link. They then choose which forensic questions to ask. The options are face swap, lip-sync or reenactment, full synthesis, and voice cloning. For X links, version 2.1.1 also shows where the video came from and gives a short summary after preprocessing.

**Reading the results.** The app runs a set of detectors and shows the results second by second. For each question, it shows which methods support the answer and how much each one contributes.

Behind the interface:

- **Pluggable detection methods.** Each detector is a self-contained module that declares what it needs and what it detects. New models show up in the app without changes to the core system. Current methods include in-house models (EfficientNet, F3Net, and TimeSformer variants) along with external detection services.
- **Shared preprocessing.** Face detection, tracking, and cropping run once per file and feed every vision model.
- **Scalable backend.** A Flask API and a gRPC broker hand work to GPU, CPU, and external-API workers. Postgres stores jobs, object storage holds media, and everything runs in Docker.
- **Weighted evidence.** Specialized detectors count more than general ones toward a verdict. Every score can be traced back to the method and the moment that produced it.

Stack: Python, Flask, gRPC, React, PyTorch, Postgres, Docker, Cloudflare.

## Community contributions

- **Misinformation Combat Alliance, Deepfakes Analysis Unit (India).** The DAU runs a public WhatsApp tipline for suspected AI-generated audio and video. It sends hard cases to detection and forensic partners for expert analysis. We contribute deepfake analyses to the DAU, and RIT reports that the unit's head, Pamposh Raina, often reaches out to our team for an expert view on suspected manipulation.
- **LEVA (Law Enforcement and Emergency Services Video Association).** LEVA provides forensic video training and certification. We led a workshop on deepfake detection for its members.
- **SWGDE (Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence).** SWGDE brings together law enforcement, academic, and commercial organizations to develop consensus standards, guidelines, and best practices for digital and multimedia evidence. We help draft its position documents, guides, and specification documents.
