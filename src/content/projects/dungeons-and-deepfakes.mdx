## Overview

Dungeons & Deepfakes (D&DF) is a scenario-based role-play methodology and interactive training platform designed to study and improve how journalists verify AI-generated media under real deadline pressure. The project adapts mechanics from tabletop role-playing games — roles, a Dungeon Master (DM), structured action choices, and cost-triggered events — into a rigorous HCI research framework, then extends that framework into a scalable digital training environment.

The core insight is simple but easy to miss in lab settings: how a journalist behaves when a tool gives them a confident-sounding answer at 11pm before a story goes live is very different from how they answer a survey about verification best practices. D&DF creates a controlled but immersive environment that captures the former.

---

## The Problem

Existing evaluations of deepfake detection tools rely heavily on static usability tasks or self-report surveys. Neither captures the interplay of **deadline pressure**, **role-based responsibility**, and **tool automation bias** that shapes real newsroom decisions. At the same time, training journalists to resist overreliance on detection tools requires more than lectures about model limitations — it requires practice in contexts that replicate the stakes.

There was also a tooling gap: manually administering role-play sessions placed enormous cognitive load on the researcher acting as DM, required deep domain expertise to improvise plausible responses to unexpected participant actions, and made consistent, granular data collection difficult. Both the research methodology and any downstream training application needed a scalable solution.

## Phase 1 — The Role-Play Study (CHI 2024)

### Framework Design

The D&DF study framework draws on the **Serious Game Design Assessment (SGDA)** model, ensuring coherence between game mechanics, research purpose, narrative fiction, and visual presentation. Each session was delivered through a structured slide deck managed by a trained Dungeon Master, who guided participants through scenarios, played non-player characters, and adjudicated invented actions — all while maintaining experimental rigor.

Participants assumed the role of investigative journalists at fictional but realistically described news organizations. Seven scenarios were developed, blending contemporary events across North American and international settings, with high-profile figures whose known behavioral patterns created natural verification challenges. Adversaries in the scenarios employed layered disinformation tactics: deepfake video, spear phishing, compromised accounts, and spoofed sources — mirroring real-world campaigns documented during the Russia-Ukraine conflict and South Korean political crises.

### The Verification Cycle

Each scenario followed a structured progression:

1. **Role introduction** — journalist context, organizational resources, and a synthetic character profile
2. **Scenario briefing** — a potentially manipulated video with referrer context and metadata
3. **Initial verdict** — rated on a 5-point scale from *Real* to *Fake*
4. **Action-response loop** — participants chose from a predefined action grid (regular, technical, goal) or invented their own; each action carried a credit cost that triggered scenario events when thresholds were crossed
5. **Verdict updates** — participants revised their assessment after each action, creating a decision trajectory
6. **Publication decision** — publish as real, publish as fake, or hold the story
7. **Debrief** — participants were shown their action patterns and discussed automation bias, tool limitations, and verification workflow improvements

A **group session variant** accommodated teams of 3–4 journalists, introducing collaborative dynamics, role-based hierarchies, and consensus requirements that revealed how social pressure and peer expertise influence tool interpretation.

### Study Sample & Ethics

A total of **34 professional journalists** participated — 24 from the United States (recruited via journalism networks and fact-checking organizations) and 10 from Bangladesh (recruited via snowball sampling in Dhaka, with compensation). Participants included fact-checkers, digital verification specialists, and general-assignment reporters. The study was approved by the RIT Institutional Review Board (IRB/HSRO), and all participants provided informed consent with an optional name-disclosure component.

### Key Findings

The study uncovered several cross-cutting patterns:

- **Automation bias** was common, particularly in time-sensitive scenarios where tool confidence scores were treated as definitive rather than probabilistic
- **Confirmation bias** emerged when participants' initial intuitions about a source's credibility shaped how they interpreted ambiguous tool outputs
- **Cross-cultural divergence**: US journalists prioritized contextual verification (source credibility, platform provenance) reflecting stronger institutional trust, while Bangladeshi journalists leaned more heavily on AI tool outputs amid broader skepticism toward official sources
- **Group dynamics** amplified both good and problematic behaviors — teams could distribute verification labor effectively, but peer pressure sometimes suppressed skepticism from individual members
- Participants overwhelmingly rated the methodology positively and, unprompted, suggested it could be adapted as a **newsroom training tool**

## Phase 2 — The Training Platform

### From Manual to Digital

The manual D&DF study validated the methodology but exposed operational limits that prevented deployment at scale: a human DM was required for every session, scenario creation was labor-intensive, and detailed behavioral logging was inconsistent. The training platform was built to address each of these directly.

### MaGe — AI Scenario Material Generator

**MaGe** is a stateful, multi-stage LLM pipeline that generates complete, pedagogically-structured training scenarios from high-level seed inputs. Rather than end-to-end generation, MaGe separates **pedagogical specification** from **narrative elaboration**:

1. A researcher provides a *Functional Seed* (narrative parameters: location, adversary type, tone) and a *Logical Seed* (gameplay constraints: action count, difficulty)
2. The LLM generates a high-level narrative with a ground truth and a set of *Scenario Directives*
3. Subsequent stages generate entities (characters, organizations), actions, events, and debrief questions — each stage informed by all previous outputs via a central `GenerationSession` object
4. All components are assembled into a single `playable-scenario.json` file consumed by the web application

MaGe integrates with **OpenAI GPT**, **Google Gemini 2.5 Flash**, and **Perplexity Sonar Pro**, with Pydantic schema enforcement ensuring structured JSON output regardless of the underlying model. Each action is tagged with a *pedagogical goal term* — **Clarify Truth**, **Introduce Ambiguity**, **Present Distractor**, **Provide Counter-Evidence**, or **Escalate Stakes** — that drives both action-response generation and post-scenario feedback scoring.

### Scenario Quality Evaluation

MaGe's output was evaluated through a two-stage framework combining LLM-as-judge scoring and a **human evaluation study with 19 journalism students**. Human participants rated scenarios across 10 quality metrics on a 1–5 Likert scale. Results showed:

| Metric | Mean Score | SD |
|---|---|---|
| Complexity | 4.40 | 0.80 |
| Clarity | 4.35 | 0.72 |
| Plausibility / Authenticity | 4.32 | 0.95 |
| Critical Thinking Stimulation | 4.00 | — |
| Investigative Richness | 3.93 | 1.05 |
| **Overall Score** | **4.13** | **0.70** |

A notable finding was the **"Optimism Gap"**: AI evaluator personas rated scenarios systematically higher than human students on every metric *except* complexity — where students rated scenarios as significantly more cognitively demanding than the LLM estimated (human M = 4.42 vs. LLM M = 4.01, Cohen's *d* = −2.31). This confirms that LLM self-evaluation cannot serve as a proxy for learner workload and that human oversight of generated content remains essential.

### The Interactive Platform

The DDF Training Platform is a **React single-page application** (TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS, Zustand) backed by a **FastAPI** server. The platform digitizes the core DDF mechanics into five phases managed by a finite state machine:

- **Briefing** — scenario context, assigned journalist role, and media artifact
- **Pre-Verification** — initial verdict before any investigative steps
- **Verification Loop** — predefined actions (with credit costs), custom action flow (MaGe generates a context-aware DM response in real time), and verdict updates after each action
- **Publication** — final decision when credits are exhausted or the trainee is confident
- **Results Page** — post-scenario reflection showing:
  - **Verdict Drift graph**: verdict trajectory plotted against actions taken and ground truth
  - **Action-level evaluation**: each action scored *good / not-good / neutral* relative to its pedagogical goal and the trainee's verdict movement
  - **Credit trajectory** and time-per-action
  - **Learning objective quiz** with targeted feedback

Every trainee interaction is written as an immutable log record, enabling both the results page feedback and downstream research analysis of verification behavior patterns.

## Status & Collaboration

The project is **ongoing**. Current priorities include:

- Expanding the scenario library across more geographic and media contexts
- Implementing the **Human Approval Gate** — an interactive review step allowing instructors to refine AI-generated scenarios before deployment
- Developing **adaptive debriefing** that generates personalized reflection questions based on each trainee's specific decision path
- Extending the framework to domains beyond journalism — including **intelligence analysis**, **law enforcement**, and **cybersecurity** contexts where verification under time pressure and automation bias pose similar risks

**I am actively looking to collaborate with educators in investigative journalism** who want to integrate D&DF scenarios into their curricula, and with researchers exploring how this methodology could transfer to other professional contexts where AI-assisted verification is becoming operationally critical.
