## Overview

Dungeons & Deepfakes (D&DF) is a three-phase research project spanning a formative needs study with professional journalists, a CHI 2024 behavioral study using a novel role-play methodology, and the design and evaluation of a digital training platform. The central challenge it addresses is methodological: existing lab studies and surveys fail to capture how journalists actually behave when a tool delivers a confident-sounding verdict minutes before a story goes live. D&DF creates a controlled but ecologically valid environment for observing those behaviors — and then a scalable system for training journalists to navigate them more effectively.

The name reflects the approach: each session casts participants as journalists working a breaking story, guided by a Dungeon Master who manages narrative progression, adjudicates invented actions, and maintains experimental rigor — directly adapted from tabletop RPG structure into a research protocol.

---

## Motivation

Three interconnected gaps motivated this work. First, deepfake detection tools are built for technical performance without accounting for how journalists embed them in high-pressure, deadline-driven verification workflows. Second, no reproducible framework existed for evaluating these tools in realistic, ecologically valid scenarios with professional practitioners across different cultural contexts. Third, journalists in resource-constrained settings lack not just tool access but the structured, deliberate practice needed to develop calibrated, skeptical judgment about what tool outputs actually mean.

D&DF addresses all three: the role-play methodology provides the evaluation framework, the cross-cultural study generates the empirical findings, and the training platform translates both into scalable professional education.

---

## Research Questions

**Phase 2 — Role-Play Study (CHI 2024)**

- **RQ2.1** — How do journalists perceive and trust deepfake detection tools across different newsroom contexts?
- **RQ2.2** — What factors influence journalists' decisions to use or not use detection tools in their verification workflows?
- **RQ2.3** — How do detection tool outputs influence journalists' decision-making processes and potential cognitive biases?
- **RQ2.4** — How effective is scenario-based role-play as a methodology for studying journalists' interactions with verification tools?

**Phase 3 — Training Platform**

- **RQ3.1** — To what extent do LLM-generated scenarios possess the quality and appropriateness needed for journalistic verification training?
- **RQ3.2** — How does the D&DF methodology, when embodied in an interactive simulation platform, shape the user experience for journalism trainees?
- **RQ3.3** — What are the perceived learning outcomes for students engaging with the D&DF platform?

## Phase 1 — Role-Play Study

### Framework Design

The D&DF study framework adapts three core tabletop RPG mechanics — character roles, a Dungeon Master managing narrative and adjudicating actions, and a cost-based action system with triggered events — into a research protocol analyzed through the **Serious Game Design Assessment (SGDA)** model. SGDA ensures coherence between game mechanics, research purpose, narrative fiction, and visual presentation, and is used here to characterize where that coherence is stronger (mechanics ↔ purpose ↔ framing) versus deliberately weaker (aesthetics ↔ content, owing to the text-based scenario delivery).

Seven fictional scenarios were developed blending contemporary events across North American and international settings. Each featured a layered disinformation threat — deepfake video combined with supporting adversarial tactics such as spear phishing, compromised accounts, and spoofed sources — mirroring real-world campaigns documented during the Russia-Ukraine conflict and South Korean political crises. Each session followed a structured verification cycle: role introduction → scenario briefing → initial verdict → action-response loop → verdict updates → publication decision → debrief.

A **group session variant** accommodated teams of 3–4 journalists, introducing collaborative dynamics, role-based hierarchies, and consensus-building requirements that revealed how peer pressure and distributed expertise interact with tool interpretation.

### Participants & Study Design

| Group | N | Region | Format |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual reporters | 19 | United States | Solo sessions |
| Group sessions | 15 | United States | Teams of 3–4 |
| Individual reporters | 10 | Bangladesh (Dhaka) | Solo sessions |
| **Total** | **34** | **US & Bangladesh** | **Mixed** |

Participants required a minimum of two years of experience in news verification or fact-checking. US recruitment used professional journalism networks and fact-checking organizations; Bangladesh used snowball sampling through professional networks, with $10 compensation. The study was approved by RIT's Institutional Review Board (HSRO). Sessions were recorded via Zoom (online) or audio capture (in-person), transcribed using Zoom, Rev.ai, and WhisperX, and supplemented with researcher field notes. Participants used a think-aloud protocol throughout.

### Analysis

Two researchers independently developed codebooks through open coding of session transcripts, merged them through axial coding, and derived higher-level themes around tool perception, trust dynamics, workflow integration, and cognitive bias. Bangladesh analysis extended the original codebook with one researcher supported by LLM-assisted review to prevent analytical drift. Decision trajectories were visualized as step graphs using the deepfake detection tool action as a reference point, capturing verification steps taken before and after tool use alongside verdict shifts and final publication decisions.

## Key Findings

### Tool Perception & Trust

All participants held broadly positive views of deepfake detection tools, but trust was consistently mediated by perceived developer credibility — particularly for US journalists, who weighted academic and neutral-institution provenance heavily. Participants with prior experience using technical verification tools engaged more critically with tool outputs; less experienced participants, including many from Bangladesh, were more likely to accept top-level scores at face value while simultaneously expressing the most interest in learning how tools worked. Time spent interpreting tool results varied substantially with no consistent pattern: some participants based decisions on a single confidence score; others carefully parsed sub-analyses.

### Automation Bias & Confirmation Bias

Automation bias appeared most strongly in time-sensitive scenarios where tool confidence scores were interpreted as definitive rather than probabilistic. Confirmation bias emerged when participants' prior assessments of source credibility shaped how they interpreted ambiguous outputs — a pattern amplified in group sessions where early consensus sometimes foreclosed individual skepticism. Group settings created a productive tension: teams could distribute verification labor effectively, but social dynamics occasionally suppressed dissent from members who noticed discrepancies.

### Cross-Cultural Divergence

US journalists prioritized contextual verification — source credibility, platform provenance, institutional trust chains — reflecting stronger baseline trust in official structures. Bangladeshi journalists relied more heavily on AI tool outputs, partly due to skepticism toward official sources and a stated desire to learn tools they lacked prior access to. This divergence directly informs how uncertainty and confidence scores should be communicated in detection interfaces across different media ecosystems.

### Methodology Effectiveness

Participants provided overwhelmingly positive feedback; the gamified structure was cited as a meaningful engagement mechanism for busy professionals who might have declined a traditional usability study. Critically, multiple participants unprompted suggested the methodology could serve as a newsroom training tool — the direct origin of Phase 2.

## Phase 2 — Training Platform

### MaGe: AI Scenario Material Generator

**MaGe** is a stateful, multi-stage LLM pipeline that generates complete, pedagogically structured training scenarios from seed inputs. The design philosophy separates *functional generation* (high-level narrative, ground truth, scenario directives) from *logical generation* (entities, actions, events, debrief questions), with each stage fully conditioned on all prior outputs via a central `GenerationSession` object. This separation allows researchers to review and approve the narrative concept before committing computational resources to the more intensive logical generation phase.

The pedagogical framework was grounded in 28 categorized learning objectives developed from the Verification Handbook, First Draft, and the D&DF study findings, then verified in consultation with Andrea Hickerson (Professor and Dean of Journalism and New Media, University of Mississippi). Each generated action is tagged with one of five **pedagogical goal terms** — *Clarify Truth*, *Introduce Ambiguity*, *Present Distractor*, *Provide Counter-Evidence*, *Escalate Stakes* — that drive both action-response generation and post-scenario feedback scoring. These goals map directly onto the bias patterns observed in the Phase 1 study.

MaGe integrates with OpenAI GPT-5, Google Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Perplexity Sonar Pro, using Pydantic schema enforcement to guarantee structured JSON output across all models. The evaluation corpus comprised **600 generated scenarios** (200 per model), seeded through stratified sampling across all 28 learning objectives.

### Platform Architecture

The DDF Training Platform is a React single-page application (TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS, Zustand) backed by a FastAPI server, digitizing the core D&DF mechanics into five sequential phases:

1. **Briefing** — scenario context, assigned journalist role, and media artifact
2. **Pre-Verification** — initial verdict before any investigative steps
3. **Verification Loop** — predefined actions with credit costs, custom action requests (MaGe generates a real-time DM response), and verdict updates after each action
4. **Publication** — final decision when credits are exhausted or confidence is reached
5. **Results** — post-scenario reflection showing the **Verdict Drift graph** (verdict trajectory plotted against actions taken and ground truth), action-level evaluation scoring each step as *good / not-good / neutral* against its pedagogical goal, credit trajectory, and a targeted **learning objective quiz** with feedback

Every trainee interaction is written as an immutable log record, enabling both the Results page feedback and downstream behavioral research analysis.

### Scenario Evaluation Study

Nineteen journalism students (primarily upper-level undergraduates from the University of Mississippi and Louisiana State University) evaluated six MaGe-generated scenarios across ten quality dimensions on a 1–5 Likert scale. The same rubric was applied by LLM evaluator personas (Expert and Student) across the full 600-scenario corpus, enabling direct comparison.

**Human evaluation results (N = 19):**

| Quality Dimension | Mean | SD |
|---|---|---|
| Complexity | 4.40 | 0.80 |
| Clarity | 4.35 | 0.72 |
| Plausibility / Authenticity | 4.32 | 0.95 |
| Coherence | 4.00 | — |
| Engagement | 4.00 | — |
| Critical Thinking Stimulation | 4.00 | — |
| Consistency | 3.98 | 1.06 |
| Investigative Richness | 3.93 | 1.05 |
| **Overall Score** | **4.13** | **0.70** |

The most important finding was the **Optimism Gap**: LLM evaluator personas rated scenarios higher than human students on every metric except complexity — where students rated scenarios as significantly more demanding than the LLM estimated (M_human = 4.42 vs. M_LLM = 4.01, Cohen's *d* = −2.31, p < .001). Effect sizes for LLM overestimation were large to extremely large across most dimensions (e.g., Newsworthy *d* = 3.84; Consistency *d* = 3.20; Overall Score *d* = 3.32). This confirms that LLM self-evaluation cannot serve as a neutral proxy for learner experience and must be calibrated against human judgment — especially for perceived difficulty, where human experience is central to instructional design.

## Research Contributions

1. **D&DF Methodology** — A replicable, IRB-validated scenario-based role-play framework for studying professional behavior with AI verification tools under ecologically valid time pressure; designed for adaptation beyond journalism to any domain involving consequential human-AI decision-making under uncertainty
2. **Cross-Cultural Behavioral Findings** — Empirical characterization of automation bias, confirmation bias, and cross-cultural divergence in trust and verification workflow integration across US and Bangladeshi newsrooms
3. **MaGe System** — A model-agnostic, pedagogically grounded LLM pipeline for generating structured training scenarios at scale, with a two-stage evaluation framework (qualitative + programmatic) and a corpus of 600 evaluated scenarios across 28 learning objectives
4. **The Optimism Gap** — Empirical evidence that LLM self-evaluation systematically overestimates scenario quality relative to human learners, with implications for AI-assisted curriculum design across educational contexts
5. **Design Implications for Detection Interfaces** — Evidence-based guidelines for how uncertainty and confidence scores should be communicated across resource-constrained and institutionally complex media environments

---

## Limitations

- **Scenario fidelity** — Text-based scenario delivery limits ecological validity compared to real multimedia deepfake artifacts; accepted to control perceptual variables and avoid generating harmful content
- **Sample representativeness** — The US–Bangladesh comparison involved uneven sample sizes (24 vs. 10 participants); generalizability to other regions with distinct press freedom conditions, media ecosystems, or verification cultures is untested
- **Tool interface constraints** — Static screenshots of DeFake.app prevented interactive exploration during the study, likely underestimating how live interface design influences trust formation and interpretation
- **LLM evaluation reliability** — The Optimism Gap finding confirms that automated quality assessment serves as a useful screening tool but requires periodic human validation, particularly for perceived complexity and cognitive load

---

## Future Directions

- **Human Approval Gate** — Interactive instructor review of MaGe-generated scenarios before deployment, allowing curriculum-specific quality control and targeted revision
- **Adaptive debriefing** — Post-scenario reflection questions dynamically generated from each trainee's specific decision path rather than a static bank, targeting individual misconceptions
- **Cross-cultural and cross-press-freedom expansion** — Extend the D&DF framework to regions such as Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa, where political climate and press freedom level may shape tool reliance differently
- **Domain transfer** — Explore adaptation of the methodology to intelligence analysis, law enforcement, OSINT, and cybersecurity training, where verification under time pressure and automation bias pose analogous professional risks
- **Longitudinal adoption studies** — Track how tool trust and verification behavior evolve across repeated platform sessions over a semester or training cohort
