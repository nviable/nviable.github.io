## Overview

I'm a human–computer interaction and digital media forensics researcher — but I came into it from art and design, and that still shapes how I work. I co-founded and lead the **DeFake Project**, an interdisciplinary initiative across four universities building usable, trustworthy tools for verifying manipulated media.

I publish under **Saniat J. Sohrawardi**; a lot of people know me as **John**.

The thread through all of it: technology should be built around how people actually think, and it should be honest about its own limits. What I care about most is media integrity — giving people both the means and the agency to decide for themselves what to trust.

## How I got here

I grew up with an art background and went to art school, then picked up tech in undergrad. What hooked me was the friction — how often genuinely good technology fails people simply because it's too convoluted to use. So I started closing that gap: branding and web design on the side, then a final-year project that was, as far as we could tell, one of the first genuinely usable peer-to-peer private video-calling tools — customizable video blocks on a canvas, no tracking, built on then-brand-new WebRTC. Investors wanted call tracking built in; we walked away, because that defeated the entire point.

That mix — engineering, usability, and an actual sense of design — became my niche. I worked as a senior developer but kept pulling projects back toward the user, and later spent time at one of the largest online grocery marketplaces, where insisting on better UX wasn't always the fastest path to ship. My blind spot has always been jargon and chasing trends; my strength is not being boxed in by consensus, which lets me build things that don't already exist.

AI pulled me in near the end of undergrad, and a PhD turned that into digital media forensics. When my advisor pitched deepfakes and we won a grant to build tools for journalists, it was a near-perfect fit for a background like mine — usability, user studies, and machine learning in a single problem. I built interfaces that shaped how people in the space work, and models that borrowed ideas from computer vision that forensics hadn't caught up to yet — then gave a lot of that time back to the community, expanding into work with intelligence analysts and law enforcement and joining SWGDE.

## Why I care, and where I'm headed

Having lived in a lot of very different places, I've seen how differently information is trusted, framed, and manipulated from one region to the next — how culture, self-preservation, patriotism, and plain commercial interest all feed into it. Years of watching that play out through real upheaval made this feel less like a research topic and more like something worth caring about. It takes a toll to care this much; I'd still rather care than not.

Preserving media integrity and handing people the tools to verify what they see is necessary — but it isn't enough, because even true facts can be bent by a well-built narrative. So I'm increasingly drawn to **narrative intelligence**, and to the longer goal behind all of it: technology that helps people see through manipulation and trace facts back to something solid, without being quietly steered by adversaries or whoever benefits most. That's the kind of ambitious, real-world work I want to keep doing.

## How I work

In practice I combine field interviews, diary studies, workshops, usability tests, and surveys to understand how people verify things under real constraints — time pressure, organizational norms, automation bias — and then I build: prototypes, interfaces, brands, whole sites when a project needs one. I'm as comfortable running a study as I am shipping the thing it points to.
